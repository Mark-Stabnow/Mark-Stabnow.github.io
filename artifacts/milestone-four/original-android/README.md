# Warehouse Inventory App

## App Summary

This app was built to give users a simple and practical way to manage warehouse inventory from a phone or tablet. The main goal was to make everyday inventory work faster, cleaner, and less annoying. Instead of bouncing between paper notes, spreadsheets, or clunky systems, the user can log in, check stock, update quantities, add items, remove items, and stay on top of shortages right from the app.

The app was designed for people like warehouse managers, stock clerks, warehouse workers, or even small business owners who need quick access to inventory without digging through a bunch of extra nonsense. The whole point was to make inventory tracking feel more organized and more real time, while also cutting down on mistakes that happen when updates get delayed or forgotten.

## Screens and Features

To make that work, the app needed a few important screens and features. The login and account creation screen gives users a straightforward way to get into the app without overcomplicating it. The main inventory screen is really the heart of the whole thing. That screen gives the user a searchable list of items, low-stock and out-of-stock filters, a total item counter, and a quick way to add new inventory.

One part of the design I really liked was using cards for each inventory item. That just made sense to me. Each card acts like its own little container for an item and houses the functionality that goes with it. Instead of throwing information and buttons all over the screen, the card keeps the item name, location, quantity, controls, and actions grouped together in one place. I think that made the app feel a lot cleaner and easier to use. It also feels more natural because the user can look at one item and do what they need to do right there without hunting around the screen.

The item cards include quantity controls, a restock button, and a details button so users can handle common tasks quickly. I wanted the app to feel like an inventory app first, with alerts supporting it in the background instead of stealing the whole spotlight. The search bar, filters, and bottom navigation all help keep the app easy to move through. I think the design worked well because it stayed focused on what the user would actually need during a normal workday.

## Coding Process

When I worked on the coding side of the app, I tried to tackle it in chunks instead of looking at it like one giant problem. That helped a lot. I started with the main structure and then built out the database, UI screens, navigation, and interactive features piece by piece. Doing it that way made it easier to stay organized and easier to figure out what broke when something stopped working.

I also tried to keep the user in mind while coding, not just while designing. I kept asking myself what the user would probably want to do first, what should be easy to reach, and what features should stay simple instead of getting overbuilt. That approach kept the app from turning into a cluttered mess and helped me stay focused on the main purpose of the project.

That is definitely something I would use again in future projects. Breaking a project into smaller parts, testing as I go, and thinking about how the user will actually move through the app makes the whole process a lot more manageable.

## Testing and Functionality

To make sure the app actually worked, I tested the main features as I built them. That included login, account creation, loading inventory, searching for items, using filters, adding new inventory, adjusting quantities, restocking items, and checking the alert behavior. I also had to test the SMS side of things, especially what happened when permission was denied, because the app still needed to work even if the user did not want to allow text alerts.

Testing matters because something can look fine on the screen and still be broken underneath. Going feature by feature helped me catch issues early and helped me see what needed to be cleaned up or reworked. It also showed me whether the app was just technically finished or actually usable, and those are not always the same thing.

## Challenges and Innovation

One of the biggest challenges was finding the right balance between useful and crowded. Inventory apps can get messy fast because there is always more information you could throw on the screen. The problem is that once you do that, the app starts feeling cramped and harder to use. I had to figure out how to show enough information to be helpful without making it look like a junk drawer.

The place where I had to get the most creative was the main inventory screen. I wanted alerts to be visible, but I did not want them hijacking the whole app. My solution was to keep inventory management as the main focus and let alerts work more like a support feature in the background. I also liked the choice to let users change quantities right from the item card because that cut down on extra steps and made the app feel quicker and more practical.

## Strongest Area

The part of the app I feel best about is the inventory screen and the way it brings everything together. That screen shows a lot of what I learned in the course because it combines layout, navigation, data display, user interaction, and overall usability in one place. More than anything, I think it shows that I was trying to design around how a real person would use the app instead of just throwing features together because they sounded good.

Overall, I think the app turned into a solid, practical tool. It is simple on purpose, and I honestly think that helped it more than it hurt it. The card-based layout, quick controls, and clean navigation made it feel organized and user focused, which was really the whole goal from the start.
